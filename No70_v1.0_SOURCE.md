## Abstract

We study the polynomial enumerating inclusion-maximal matchings in a bouquet of equal-length cycles sharing one hub. For every integer q >= 5 and r >= 1, the proposed number of nonreal roots, counted with algebraic multiplicity, is 2(floor((q-1)/2)-floor(q/3)) floor((r-1)/2). The manuscript presents an analytical proof candidate based on a three-residue interlacing induction and a rational-function root count, accompanied by finite exact checks. This preprint has received the AI reviews described in its evidence statement; it has not undergone human peer review or formal verification. Novelty assessment: inconclusive; full-text comparison with Burnham-Erey (2025) incomplete.

## 1. Statement, normalization, and elementary cases

Let \(q\ge5\), \(r\ge1\), and let \(\mathcal B_{q,r}\) be the bouquet of \(r\) cycles of length \(q\), identified at exactly one common vertex. Its **maximal-matching polynomial** is \(Z_{q,r}(x)=\sum_M x^{|M|}\), where the sum is over *inclusion-maximal* matchings (not maximum-cardinality matchings).

Put

\[
 F_0=F_1=1,\quad F_2=x,\qquad F_n=x(F_{n-2}+F_{n-3})\quad(n\ge3),
\]

For a path on \(n\ge3\) vertices, a maximal matching either uses its first edge \(12\) (contributing \(xF_{n-2}\)) or omits \(12\), in which case it must use \(23\) (contributing \(xF_{n-3}\)); this gives the recurrence and \(F_3=2x\).
\[
 m=\lfloor q/3\rfloor,\qquad
 a=x^{2-m}F_{q-5},\quad b=2x^{1-m}F_{q-2},\quad c=x^{-m}F_{q-1},
\]
\[
 d=\left\lfloor\frac{q-1}{2}\right\rfloor-\left\lfloor\frac q3\right\rfloor,
 \qquad e=\begin{cases}1&q\text{ even},\\0&q\text{ odd}.\end{cases}
\]

Here \(a,b,c\) are **actual polynomials** (after cancellation of powers of \(x\)), with positive leading coefficients, and

\[
 Z_{q,r}(x)=x^{mr}P_{q,r}(x),\qquad
 P_{q,r}(x)=a(x)^r+r\,b(x)c(x)^{r-1}.
 \tag{1}
\]

**Rooted-petal decomposition (self-contained proof of (1)).** On each cycle label the hub by \(v\), with remaining vertices \(1,\ldots,q-1\) in cyclic order. Let \(F_n\) be the maximal-matching polynomial of the path with \(n\) vertices, with \(F_0=F_1=1\) (the empty matching of an edgeless path is maximal).

If \(v\) is unmatched, for every petal both vertices adjacent to \(v\) must be matched by edges inside that petal; otherwise the corresponding edge incident with \(v\) could be added. Thus the two path-end edges \(12\) and \((q-2)(q-1)\) are forced. The remaining \(q-5\) vertices form a path whose matching must be maximal. The per-petal contribution is
\[
  S_q(x)=x^2 F_{q-5}(x).
\]
If \(v\) is matched, choose the unique petal containing its matching edge and one of the two incident edges within that petal (two choices). Removing that edge's two endpoints leaves a path with \(q-2\) vertices which must carry a maximal matching, so the matched petal contributes
\[
  E_q(x)=2xF_{q-2}(x).
\]
In each of the remaining \(r-1\) petals, hub edges are already blocked by the chosen edge. The \(q-1\) nonhub vertices therefore form an independently maximal-matched path, with contribution
\[
  T_q(x)=F_{q-1}(x).
\]
These cases are disjoint and exhaustive, including \(r=1\) and \(q=5\). Consequently
\[
Z_{q,r}=S_q^r+rE_qT_q^{r-1}
=x^{mr}\bigl(a^r+rbc^{r-1}\bigr),
\]
by the definitions of \(a,b,c,m\) above. This is an elementary specialization of established rooted decomposition methods; **no general cut-vertex principle is claimed novel**. The degrees are
\(\deg a=\deg c=d\), \(\deg b=d+e\), and \(\deg P_{q,r}=rd+e\), with no cancellation of leading terms. Because of positive coefficients, \(a,b,c\) have no positive zeros.

**Candidate Theorem.** For every integer \(q\ge5\) and every \(r\ge1\), the number of nonreal roots of \(Z_{q,r}\), counted with algebraic multiplicity, is

\[
 \boxed{N_{\rm nonreal}(q,r)=2d\left\lfloor\frac{r-1}{2}\right\rfloor.}\tag{2}
\]

Consequently, \(Z_{q,r}\) is real-rooted iff \(r\le2\) or \(q=6\). The rest of this document presents an analytical proof **for independent scrutiny**, not a previously certified theorem.

Small-parameter anchors (the case \(q=6\) is degenerate with \(d=0\)):

| \(q\) | \(a\) | \(b\) | \(c\) | \((d,e)\) |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| 5 | \(x\) | \(4x\) | \(1+x\) | (1,0) |
| 6 | \(1\) | \(2(x+1)\) | \(3\) | (0,1) |
| 7 | \(x\) | \(6x\) | \(x+3\) | (1,0) |
| 8 | \(2x\) | \(2x(x+3)\) | \(4x+1\) | (1,1) |

## 2. Complete three-residue braid lemma, including endpoint signs

For \(k\ge0\) define

\[
 A_k=F_{3k}/x^k,\qquad B_k=F_{3k+1}/x^k,\qquad C_k=F_{3k+2}/x^{k+1}.
\]

From the \(F\)-recurrence:

\[
 A_{k+1}=A_k+B_k,\qquad B_{k+1}=B_k+xC_k,\qquad C_{k+1}=A_{k+1}+C_k,
 \tag{3}
\]

with \(A_0=B_0=C_0=1\). All coefficients are nonnegative, and all *nonzero coefficients* and leading coefficients are positive. From (3) the degrees are

- \(k=2s\): \(\deg A_k=\deg B_k=\deg C_k=s\);
- \(k=2s+1\): \(\deg A_k=\deg C_k=s\) and \(\deg B_k=s+1\).

The reason no degree drops is that every sum in (3) adds polynomials with **positive leading coefficients**; in particular, \(B_k+xC_k\) does not cancel its top-degree term even if its summands have the same degree.

**Braid lemma.** For every \(k\ge0\), every root of every nonconstant \(A_k,B_k,C_k\) is *strictly negative, real and simple*. With increasing roots \(a_i,b_i,c_i\) at the same level:

- if \(k=2s\ge2\): \(c_1<a_1<b_1<c_2<a_2<b_2<\cdots<c_s<a_s<b_s<0\);
- if \(k=2s+1\): \(b_1<c_1<a_1<b_2<c_2<a_2<\cdots<b_s<c_s<a_s<b_{s+1}<0\).

For \(s=0\), the odd chain means only \(b_1<0\); \(A_1=2,B_1=1+x,C_1=3\). The \(k=0\) braid is empty.

**Proof.** Assume the displayed braid holds at level \(k\). Write \(A'=A+B\), \(B'=B+xC\), \(C'=A'+C\) for the level-\(k+1\) polynomials, with new roots \(\alpha_i,\beta_i,\gamma_i\). For any polynomial with positive leading coefficient and only simple real roots, \(\operatorname{sgn}D(t)=(-1)^{n_D(t)}\) at a nonroot \(t\), where \(n_D(t)\) is the number of zeros of \(D\) strictly to the *right* of \(t\). This rule replaces every informal assertion that signs “alternate”. All interval endpoints used below are **strictly negative** except the explicitly stated endpoint \(0\).

**Even \(k=2s\ge2\) to odd \(k+1\).** At the ends of \((a_i,b_i)\), the values of \(A'=A+B\) have signs \((-1)^{s-i+1}\) and \((-1)^{s-i}\), respectively; hence \(a_i<\alpha_i<b_i\). At \(c_i\) and \(\alpha_i\), \(C'=A'+C\) has signs \((-1)^{s-i+1}\) and \((-1)^{s-i}\): at \(c_i\), both \(A(c_i)\) and \(B(c_i)\) have the indicated common sign, while at \(\alpha_i\), \(C'(\alpha_i)=C(\alpha_i)\). Hence \(c_i<\gamma_i<\alpha_i\).

For \(B'=B+xC\):

| Interval | Left endpoint sign | Right endpoint sign |
|---|---|---|
| \(( -\infty,c_1)\) | \((-1)^{s+1}\) (leading term of \(xC\)) | \((-1)^s\), since \(B'(c_1)=B(c_1)\) |
| \((b_{i-1},c_i)\), \(2\le i\le s\) | \((-1)^{s-i}\), since \(B'(b_{i-1})=b_{i-1}C(b_{i-1})\) | \((-1)^{s-i+1}\), since \(B'(c_i)=B(c_i)\) |
| \((b_s,0)\) | negative, since \(b_sC(b_s)<0\) | positive, since \(B'(0)=B(0)>0\) |

All signs differ, so \(B'\) has \(s+1\) distinct real roots in those disjoint intervals. The \(s\) roots of \(A'\), the \(s\) roots of \(C'\), and the \(s+1\) roots of \(B'\) exhaust their degrees; therefore there can be **no additional or repeated roots**. The following placements, retaining old roots as guideposts, are justified:

\[
 \beta_i<c_i<\gamma_i<\alpha_i<b_i<\beta_{i+1}\quad (1\le i\le s), \tag{4}
\]

where \(\beta_1<c_1\) and \(\beta_{s+1}<0\). Also \(c_i<a_i<\alpha_i\). **No relative order between \(a_i\) and \(\gamma_i\) is asserted or needed**. Deleting the old guideposts gives the odd braid.

**Odd \(k=2s+1\) to even \(k+1\).** Here \(s\) may be zero. For \(A'=A+B\), the values at \(-\infty,b_1\) have signs \((-1)^{s+1},(-1)^s\). For \((a_{i-1},b_i)\), \(2\le i\le s+1\), their signs are \((-1)^{s+2-i},(-1)^{s+1-i}\). Thus \(\alpha_1<b_1\) and \(a_{i-1}<\alpha_i<b_i\) for \(2\le i\le s+1\).

For \(B'\), at \((b_i,c_i)\) the signs are \((-1)^{s-i},(-1)^{s+1-i}\), because \(B'(b_i)=b_iC(b_i)\) and \(B'(c_i)=B(c_i)\). On \((b_{s+1},0)\), the signs are negative, positive. Hence \(b_i<\beta_i<c_i\) and \(b_{s+1}<\beta_{s+1}<0\).

For \(C'=A'+C\), at \((-\infty,\alpha_1)\) the signs are \((-1)^{s+1},(-1)^s\). On \((c_{i-1},\alpha_i)\) (\(2\le i\le s+1\)) the signs are \((-1)^{s+2-i},(-1)^{s+1-i}\), because \(C'(c_{i-1})=A'(c_{i-1})\) and \(C'(\alpha_i)=C(\alpha_i)\). Thus \(\gamma_1<\alpha_1\) and \(c_{i-1}<\gamma_i<\alpha_i\). Again the disjoint sign-change intervals exactly match all three degrees \(s+1\), excluding further or multiple roots.

The complete old-root guidepost chain contains

\[
 \gamma_i<\alpha_i<b_i<\beta_i<c_i<\gamma_{i+1}\quad (1\le i\le s),\qquad
 \gamma_{s+1}<\alpha_{s+1}<b_{s+1}<\beta_{s+1}<0.\tag{5}
\]

Additionally, \(c_i<a_i<\alpha_{i+1}\) for \(1\le i\le s\). As in (4), no unproved relative order of old \(a_i\) and new \(\gamma_{i+1}\) is required. Deleting the guideposts yields the even braid. This proof includes \(s=0\): \(A_2=3+x\), \(B_2=1+4x\), \(C_2=6+x\), whose negative roots satisfy \(-6<-3<-1/4\). The induction is complete. \(\square\)

## 3. Precise transfer to T(q) for all q, with full guidepost chains

**Hypothesis T(q).** The polynomials \(a,c\) have exactly \(d\) simple real roots and \(b\) has exactly \(d+e\) simple real roots, all nonpositive, with no common \(c\)-root. In increasing order:

- for odd \(q\): \(c_1<a_1\le b_1<c_2<a_2\le b_2<\cdots<c_d<a_d\le b_d\);
- for even \(q\), \(d\ge1\): \(b_1<c_1<a_1\le b_2<c_2<a_2\le b_3<\cdots<b_d<c_d<a_d\le b_{d+1}\).

An equality is permitted **only at the final pair \(a_d=b_{d+e}=0\)**. For \(q=6\), \(d=0\), \(b\) has one simple negative root and \(a,c\) are nonzero constants.

The normalizations in §1 and the definitions in §2 yield exactly:

| Class | \(a\) | \(b\) | \(c\) | Valid for |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| \(q=3k\) | \(B_{k-2}\) | \(2B_{k-1}\) | \(C_{k-1}\) | \(k\ge2\) |
| \(q=3k+1\) | \(xC_{k-2}\) | \(2xC_{k-1}\) | \(A_k\) | \(k\ge2\) |
| \(q=3k+2\) | \(xA_{k-1}\) | \(2xA_k\) | \(B_k\) | \(k\ge1\) |

*Case \(q=3k\).* Set \(t=k-2\). If \(t\) is even \(2s\), the even-to-odd transition (4) gives for the relevant nonzero roots
\(\beta_i<\gamma_i<b_i^{\rm old}<\beta_{i+1}\). They are respectively roots of \(b,c,a,b\), so this is the required **even-\(q\)** chain. If \(t\) is odd \(2s+1\), the odd-to-even transition (5) gives \(\gamma_i<b_i^{\rm old}<\beta_i<\gamma_{i+1}\), the **odd-\(q\)** chain. The \(t=0\) case is \(q=6\), already listed in §1.

*Case \(q=3k+2\).* Set \(t=k-1\). If \(t=2s\) is even, the even-to-odd transition gives \(\beta_i<a_i^{\rm old}<\alpha_i<\beta_{i+1}\) for \(1\le i\le s\), with \(\beta_{s+1}<0\). After appending the shared \(a,b\)-root at \(0\), this is the **odd-\(q\)** chain. If \(t=2s+1\) is odd, the odd-to-even transition gives \(\alpha_i<\beta_i<a_i^{\rm old}<\alpha_{i+1}\) for \(1\le i\le s\), and \(\alpha_{s+1}<\beta_{s+1}<0\). After appending the shared \(0\), this is the **even-\(q\)** chain. The \(s=0\) first case is \(q=5\), as in §1.

*Case \(q=3k+1\), requiring two transitions.* Set \(t=k-1\). Keep separate symbols throughout:

- \(o_i\) = roots of the **old** \(C_{t-1}\) (part of \(a=xC_{t-1}\));
- \(u_i\) = roots of the **middle** \(C_t\) (part of \(b=2xC_t\));
- \(v_i,z_i\) = roots of the **middle** \(A_t,B_t\);
- \(w_i\) = roots of the **new** \(A_{t+1}=A_t+B_t\) (equal to \(c\)).

If \(t=2s\) is **even** (\(s\ge1\)), the old-to-middle odd-to-even transition (5) and the middle-to-new even-to-odd transition (4) give, for \(1\le i\le s-1\),

\[
 u_i<v_i<w_i<z_i<o_i<u_{i+1},\qquad u_s<v_s<w_s<z_s<0.
 \tag{6}
\]

The first transition supplies \(z_i<o_i<u_{i+1}\); the second supplies \(v_i<w_i<z_i\). Therefore \(u_i<w_i<o_i<u_{i+1}\) with a final \(u_s<w_s<0\). The roots of \(a\) are \(o_1,\ldots,o_{s-1},0\); roots of \(b\) are \(u_1,\ldots,u_s,0\); roots of \(c\) are \(w_1,\ldots,w_s\). Hence this is exactly the **even-\(q\)** chain with \(d=s,e=1\).

If \(t=2s+1\) is **odd** (\(s\ge0\)), the old-to-middle even-to-odd transition (4) and middle-to-new odd-to-even transition (5) give

\[
 w_1<z_1<o_1<u_1<v_1<w_2<z_2<o_2<u_2<v_2<\cdots<w_{s}<z_s<o_s<u_s<v_s<w_{s+1}<0.
 \tag{7}
\]

Indeed the first transition supplies \(z_i<o_i<u_i<v_i<z_{i+1}\), while the second puts \(w_1<z_1\) and \(v_i<w_{i+1}<z_{i+1}\). Dropping the middle guideposts yields \(w_i<o_i<u_i<w_{i+1}\). The roots of \(a\) are \(o_1,\ldots,o_s,0\), the roots of \(b\) are \(u_1,\ldots,u_s,0\), and the roots of \(c\) are \(w_1,\ldots,w_{s+1}\). Thus this is the **odd-\(q\)** chain with \(d=s+1,e=0\). For \(s=0\), (7) is only \(w_1<0\), corresponding to \(q=7\) in §1.

This covers all \(q\ge5\). In the classes \(q\equiv1,2\pmod3\), both \(a,b\) contain a single factor \(x\), while \(c(0)>0\), and all *other* roots are negative and distinct. If \(q\equiv0\pmod3\), there is no shared \(a,b\)-zero. Therefore **T(q) holds for every \(q\ge5\)**, conditional only on the elementary braid proof in §2. \(\square\)

## 4. Full conditional root-counting proof from v0.8 (integrated, not merely referenced)

We now prove (2) assuming T(q), without requiring finite \(q\)-certificates. The braid in §§2–3 then supplies T(q) for all \(q\).

### 4.1. Rational functions and monotonicity

Let \(d\ge1\) and set \(g=a/c\), \(h=b/c\) wherever \(c\ne0\). Since \(a,b,c\) have positive leading coefficients and their negative roots satisfy T(q), each simple pole \(c_i\) has the **strictly negative** residues

\[
 \frac{a(c_i)}{c'(c_i)}<0,\qquad \frac{b(c_i)}{c'(c_i)}<0.
 \tag{8}
\]

For the odd chain, this follows by counting the \(d-i+1\) zeros of both \(a,b\) right of \(c_i\), versus \(d-i\) zeros right of the derivative sign of \(c\). For the even chain, \(b\) has **exactly \(i\) zeros to the left** of \(c_i\), namely \(b_1,\ldots,b_i\), and \(d+1-i\) zeros to the right of \(c_i\); \(a\) has \(d+1-i\) zeros right; again the signs differ from \(c'(c_i)\). Thus all relevant residues are negative.

Partial fractions now give \(g'(x)>0\), \(h'(x)>0\) on every interval free of \(c\)-poles: the polynomial part of \(g\) is constant, and that of \(h\) is constant when \(e=0\) or affine with strictly positive slope when \(e=1\). Moreover every *uncancelled* pole of \(a/b\) has strictly **positive** residue by the corresponding root order; its polynomial part is constant if \(e=0\), and zero if \(e=1\). Consequently

\[
 \bigl(a/b\bigr)'\le0\quad\text{wherever }b\ne0,
 \tag{9}
\]

and it is strictly negative except when \(a/b\) is a constant rational function (when the derivative vanishes identically). No equality case is needed below. If \(a,b\) share the last root \(0\), the ratio \(a/b\) is extended through \(0\) by cancelling their common simple factor. This changes neither (9) nor any interval of interest. These observations are elementary rational-function facts, not assertions of a new interlacing theorem.

For \(r\ge2\), at any \(c_i\), \(P_{q,r}(c_i)=a(c_i)^r\ne0\). For \(r=1\), equation (8) gives \((a(c_i)+b(c_i))/c'(c_i)<0\), so \(P_{q,1}(c_i)=a(c_i)+b(c_i)\ne0\) as well. Thus all real roots of \(P\) are precisely the zeros, away from poles, of

\[
 H_r(x)=\frac{P_{q,r}(x)}{c(x)^r}=g(x)^r+r h(x).
 \tag{10}
\]

### 4.2. Odd \(r\): exactly \(d+e\) simple real roots

For odd \(r\), \(r-1\) is even and

\[
 H_r'=r g^{r-1}g'+r h'>0
\]

on every pole interval (including \(r=1\)). Immediately to the **right** of each \(c_i\), both \(g,h\to-\infty\); immediately to the **left** of \(c_{i+1}\), both \(g,h\to+\infty\). Hence there is one simple root of \(H_r\) in each of the \(d-1\) bounded pole intervals. The rightmost interval \((c_d,+\infty)\) contributes exactly one more root: the left endpoint gives \(-\infty\), while at \(+\infty\) both \(g\) and \(h\) have positive limiting value (or \(h\to+\infty\) if \(e=1\)).

If \(e=0\), the far-left interval \((-\infty,c_1)\) has \(g,h>0\) throughout, so it contributes no root. If \(e=1\), \(g>0\) throughout it; \(h(x)\to-\infty\) as \(x\to-\infty\), while \(H_r\to+\infty\) as \(x\to c_1^-\). Strict monotonicity gives exactly one additional simple root. Thus the total is \((d-1)+1+e=d+e\) distinct simple real roots. When \(a=b=0\), the only permitted common root is \(x=0\), which lies in the **rightmost pole interval** \((c_d,+\infty)\). In that interval, strict monotonicity of \(H_r\) already counts \(0\) as its unique root; it is **not** counted as an extra root.

### 4.3. Even \(r\): exactly \(2d+e\) simple real roots

Here \(r\ge2\). On each interval beginning at \(c_i\), let \(\tilde b_i=b_{i+e}\) be the \(b\)-root after the corresponding \(a_i\); the order is
\(c_i<a_i\le\tilde b_i\), followed by \(c_{i+1}\) (or \(+\infty\) for \(i=d\)). On \((c_i,a_i)\), both \(g,h<0\), and equation (10) is equivalent to

\[
 (-g)^{r-1}\,(a/b)=r. \tag{11}
\]

As \(x\) increases, \(-g(x)>0\) strictly decreases and \(a/b>0\) is nonincreasing by (9). Thus the left side is **strictly decreasing**. Its limit at \(c_i^+\) is \(+\infty\), and its limit at \(a_i^-\) is \(0\). This remains true when \(a_i=\tilde b_i=0\): cancellation makes \(a/b\) approach a finite positive limit while \(-g\to0\). Therefore precisely one simple zero lies in \((c_i,a_i)\).

If \(a_i<\tilde b_i\), then on \((a_i,\tilde b_i)\), \(g>0,h<0\) and \(H_r'>0\). The endpoint signs are \(H_r(a_i)=r h(a_i)<0\) and \(H_r(\tilde b_i)=g(\tilde b_i)^r>0\); hence there is precisely one additional simple zero. If instead \(a_i=\tilde b_i=0\) (permitted only for the last pair), then \(P(0)=0\) and this root is **simple**: for even \(r\ge2\), since \(b'(0)>0,c(0)>0\),

\[
 P'(0)=r b'(0)c(0)^{r-1}>0.
\]

For \(r=1\), which belongs to §4.2, \(P'(0)=a'(0)+b'(0)>0\). Neither case creates a repeated real root. Beyond \(\tilde b_i\) and before the next pole, or to \(+\infty\), both \(g,h>0\), hence there are no further roots. The \(d\) right-of-pole intervals therefore give **exactly two** simple real roots each.

If \(e=0\), the far-left interval contributes none since \(g,h>0\). If \(e=1\), there is exactly one additional root there: \(g>0\), so \(H_r'>0\), with \(H_r(-\infty)=-\infty\) (\(h\) has positive linear coefficient) and \(H_r(c_1^-)=+\infty\). Thus \(P\) has precisely \(2d+e\) distinct simple real roots.

### 4.4. The \(d=0\) exception and algebraic multiplicity

The only \(q\ge5\) with \(d=0\) is \(q=6\). Here \(a=1\), \(b=2(x+1)\), \(c=3\), so

\[
 P_{6,r}(x)=1+2r3^{r-1}(x+1),
\]

which has exactly one simple real root for every \(r\). This equals \(d+e=2d+e=1\).

For all \(q\), \(\deg P=rd+e\). Subtracting the real-root counts gives

\[
 \begin{cases}
 (r-1)d,&r\text{ odd},\\
 (r-2)d,&r\text{ even},
 \end{cases}
 =2d\lfloor(r-1)/2\rfloor.
\]

All counted real roots of \(P\) are simple. Multiplication by \(x^{mr}\) adds only the real root \(0\), possibly with higher multiplicity, and changes no nonreal-root count. Finally, \(d=0\) only when \(q=6\), while \(d>0\) for all other \(q\ge5\). Equation (2) and the real-rootedness criterion follow. \(\square\)
